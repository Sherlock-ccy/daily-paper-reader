ABSTRACT
We introduce Gen4P, a unified framework for recovering 4D geometry and motion
from monocular video. We show that a pretrained multi-step video generator
can be repurposed into a feed-forward 4D perceiver without iterative denoising.
Building on on-demand querying, Gen4P processes clean video latents in a single
encoder pass and predicts the requested 4D attributes through a lightweight cross-
attention decoder. The on-demand query formulation enables flexible spatial
sampling, and our proposed continuous-time queries further provide temporal
flexibility. To strengthen geometric knowledge, we propose a straightforward
plug-in solution by incorporating a pretrained video-depth LoRA as a frozen
geometry prior, improving overall performance without adding extra trainable
parameters. Gen4P achieves state-of-the-art or competitive performance on tasks
of 3D point tracking, video depth and camera pose estimation. Qualitative results
on in-the-wild videos illustrate its capability of tracking complex motion while
maintaining the delicate structure. Together, these results demonstrate the potential
of video generative pretraining for unified 4D perception. See https://gen4p.
github.io/ for more visual results.

INTRODUCTION
We consider the problem of feed-forward full 4D reconstruction from monocular video. A unified
4D representation of scene geometry, depth, camera poses, and 3D point trajectories provides a
foundation for simulating and interacting with the physical environment. However, recovering such a
representation remains challenging: it requires accurately capturing both the static geometry and the
dynamic motion of the scene across viewpoints and over time.
Recent advances in feed-forward reconstruction demonstrate the value of pretrained foundational
vision models. Geometry Foundation Models (GFMs), including VGGT (Wang et al., 2025a), VGGT-
Ω(Wang et al., 2026a), and DA3 (Lin et al., 2025), build on pretrained vision models (Oquab et al.,
2023; Sim´eoni et al., 2025), adapting them through large-scale geometric supervision. These GFMs

then provide strong geometry priors for subsequent 4D reconstruction: V-DPM (Sucar et al., 2026)
and 4RC (Luo et al., 2026), for instance, extend VGGT and DA3 to jointly recover geometry and
motion. However, geometry priors alone do not explicitly model how points should evolve over time
in dynamic scenes.
Meanwhile, foundational generative models offer a complementary source of priors over appearance,
geometry, and motion, by learning to synthesize images and videos (Wan et al., 2025; ByteDance
Seed Team, 2026). Recent studies have exploited generative priors for 2D correspondence (Hedlin
et al., 2023), depth estimation (Ke et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2025), 3D geometry recovery (Zhang et al.,
2025b; Lu et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025), motion tracking (Zhu et al., 2026; Nam et al., 2026a),
and general perception (Wang et al., 2026b). However, they still address 4D perception through
task-specific approaches. Besides, they often require dense, frame-aligned supervision, limiting their
flexibility in handling arbitrary spatiotemporal video inputs.
In this work, we introduce Gen4P, a 4D perceiver that leverages rich priors from large-scale pretrained
video generators for full 4D reconstruction. Specifically, Gen4P tames a multi-step video generator
into a single-step feed-forward 4D perceiver. To this end, the objective is formulated as learning a
direct regression network, instead of the iterative denoising for geometric reconstruction in prior
works (Ke et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2025). Such a formulation requires only one
encoder forward pass per input clip. The resulting scene tokens are then shared across queries,
reducing the encoding cost over repeated 4D predictions.
We obtain the full 4D attributes by querying the scene tokens using a lightweight cross-attention
decoder. It decouples the output sampling from pre-extracted feature grids, allowing spatial flexibility
in the output resolution. Additionally, we introduce a continuous-time query mechanism to handle
variable-length video sequences effectively. Furthermore, we discover that a pretrained video-depth
LoRA can further be incorporated as a frozen plug-in geometric prior, strengthening geometry without
introducing additional trainable parameters.
Our key contributions are summarized as follows: (i) we repurpose a video generator into a feed-
forward 4D perceiver under sparse query supervision, offering an alternative to GFM-based methods;
(ii) we introduce a continuous-time query mechanism together with on-demand decoding to provide
temporal and spatial flexibility; (iii) we incorporate a frozen depth LoRA as a plug-in geometry
prior, improving overall 4D reconstruction without adding trainable parameters; and (iv) we provide
a comprehensive evaluation of our method on a set of 4D reconstruction tasks, complemented by
qualitative results on in-the-wild videos, demonstrating its effectiveness and generalization.

RELATED WORK
Feed-forward GFMs directly regress pixel-aligned geometry, from pairwise point maps (Wang
et al., 2024) to sequence models that use spatial memory (Wang & Agapito, 2025), recurrent scene
states (Wang et al., 2025b), or joint multi-view attention with multiple prediction heads (Wang et al.,
2025a). Later models remove the reference view (Wang et al., 2026c), accept optional geometric
inputs (Keetha et al., 2026), or learn unified depth–ray targets (Lin et al., 2025).
Point Tracking in 2D and 3D. 2D point tracking has been extensively studied in both sparse and
dense settings (Doersch et al., 2022; Karaev et al., 2024; 2025; Lai et al., 2026). 3D point tracking
methods lift pixels with monocular depth and iterative refinement (Xiao et al., 2024), improve dense
long-range estimation through optimized surrogate queries (Ngo et al., 2025b;a), or jointly estimate
geometry, camera, and object motion (Xiao et al., 2025). GFM-based 4D perceivers represent dynamic
3D scenes through per-frame point maps (Zhang et al., 2025a), dynamic point maps (Sucar et al.,
2026), conditional frame-wise decoding (Luo et al., 2026), or motion bases (Lin et al., 2026). Other
approaches include common-time point-map pairs (Feng et al., 2025), parametric trajectories (Liu
et al., 2026), and scene flow (Zhu et al., 2026; Karhade et al., 2026; Qian et al., 2026). Most rely
on frame-aligned grid decoding, coupling output sampling and cost to spatial resolution, whereas
D4RT (Zhang et al., 2026a) decouples prediction from predefined grids through on-demand queries.

and motion priors transferable to perception tasks (Zhan et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2026). Image
adaptations estimate depth through iterative denoising (Ke et al., 2024) or single-step regression (He
et al., 2025). Video adaptations predict depth (Hu et al., 2025), point maps (Xu et al., 2025), fused

METHODS
Given an input video V = {It}T

predict a full set of 4D attributes, including 3D tracking, 3D point clouds, video depths, and camera
parameters. Instead of training a large-scale 4D geometric network from scratch, in Gen4P we
repurpose a pretrained video generator ppre as a feed-forward 4D perceiver.
As illustrated in Fig. 2, Gen4P adopts an on-demand query-based formulation inspired by D4RT
(Sec. 3.1). A lightweight decoder queries spatiotemporal features from the pretrained video generator
(Sec. 3.2.1) to predict 4D attributes at specified locations and times. Since real-world videos vary
in duration, a practical 4D reconstruction method should accommodate different sequence lengths.
Unlike D4RT, we introduce a continuous time query (Sec. 3.2.2) to enhance flexibility. We further
introduce a plug-in geometry prior (Sec. 3.3) to enhance geometric performance. The training
objective is described in Sec. 3.4. We detail each component below.

3.2
VIDEO GENERATORS AS 4D PERCEIVERS
Gen4P repurposes a video generator into a single-pass feed-forward 4D perceiver, without multi-step
denoising (Ke et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025) or noise injection (Zhan et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2026)
to read out diffusion features. This formulation enables direct feature extraction while avoiding the
overhead of iterative denoising. Specifically, Gen4P consists of a video-generator-based encoder with
a LoRA-adapted video DiT backbone, and a cross-attention query decoder, as described next.

variable lengths. Rather than using discrete lookup tables for a fixed 48-frame horizon (Zhang et al.,
2026a), we introduce a continuous temporal parameterization that allows the model to operate on

Cross-Attention Decoder. The decoder consists of multiple cross-attention blocks, where the query
attends to the scene tokens F at each layer. We construct the query embedding q from the continuous
time query embeddings esrc, etgt, and ecam, the 2D position embedding ϕuv(u, v) and the RGB
patch embedding ϕpatch(pu,v). Then we feed q and F into a cross-attention decoder D to obtain the
query feature zq, which is further mapped by an MLP head H to the complete set of 4D attributes, as
defined in Eqs. (3) and (4).
3.3
PLUG-IN GEOMETRY PRIOR
Recent GFM-finetuned 4D perceivers (Sucar et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026) benefit
from geometry priors, which are learned through large-scale dense 3D pretraining. In contrast,
Gen4P adapts a pretrained video generator to exploit its motion prior, while its sparse query-based
supervision alone provides a weaker signal for learning geometry than the GFM-finetuned methods.
To narrow this gap, we propose a natural solution within our framework: by plugging in a pretrained
video-depth adapter as a frozen geometry prior, we are able to reuse knowledge learned from dense
depth estimation without a separate dense 3D pretraining stage.
Double-LoRA Architecture. To incorporate pretrained dense geometric knowledge, we adopt a
plug-in geometry prior from a pretrained video depth estimation model, DVD (Zhang et al., 2026b),
which is also built upon Wan2.1. We combine the pretrained Depth-LoRA (∆WDepth) with our
4D-LoRA (∆W4D) (Sec. 3.2.1), forming a double-LoRA architecture. For each adapted layer, the
effective weight is

Lightweight Training. Following prior work on retaining pretrained adapters during task adapta-
tion (Yu et al., 2025), we freeze the VAE encoder, WDiT and ∆WDepth, and jointly optimize ∆W4D,
the query embeddings, the query decoder, and the prediction head. Thus, the pretrained depth update
is retained while a separate update is learned for 4D perception under the task objective. We point
out that the double-LoRA variant requires no additional dense prediction head and has the same
number of trainable parameters as the variant using 4D-LoRA alone. In Sec. 4.5, we compare several
adaptation strategies, providing empirical support for our design.

TRAINING OBJECTIVE
Our method predicts a full set of 4D attributes and is trained with a composite loss:
L = λ3DL3D + λconfLconf + λ2DL2D + λvisLvis + λdispLdisp + λnormalLnormal,
(8)
where L3D, Lconf, L2D, Lvis, Ldisp, and Lnormal denote the 3D position, confidence, 2D position,

Data Usage. We distinguish two types of input frames: encoder frames, which are input to the
encoder to extract scene tokens, and query frames, which provide local RGB patches for decoder
queries. They share the same aspect ratio but may differ in resolution. Encoder frames are maximally
center-cropped to one of five predefined aspect ratios and uniformly resized to the largest resolution
within a 480 × 832 bound, while query frames generally retain their original resolution. To speed up
training, we use EVAE to pre-encode all video clips into latents and discard the VAE during training.
We also apply temporal augmentation to handle varying motion speeds. To this end, each raw video
is subsampled into 49-frame clips with temporal intervals ranging from 1 to 4. During training, we
randomly slice the first 21 ∼49 frames in steps of 4, and head-truncate the video latent accordingly.
We also apply downsizing-only spatial augmentation to the query frames, so that the model sees a
variety of query-patch fields of view. See Secs. B.1 and B.2 for more details about datasets and data
preprocessing & sampling, respectively.
Architecture. We evaluate two adaptation variants. The basic variant, denoted as ‘Ours’, leverages
motion priors from a large-scale video generator, with a trainable rank-512 4D-LoRA, ∆W 512
4D . The
double-LoRA variant, denoted as ‘Ours-II’, additionally incorporates geometry priors through the
frozen rank-512 Depth-LoRA, ∆W 512
Depth (Sec. 3.3). Both variants have 0.70B trainable encoder
parameters. We use an 8-block cross-attention decoder, which has 0.18B trainable parameters.
Optimization. We train for 75K optimization steps using AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017) on 8
NVIDIA H200 GPUs. Gradient accumulation yields an effective batch size of 32 video clips. The
learning rate is linearly warmed up to a peak of 10−4 over the first 2.5K steps and then follows a
cosine decay schedule to 10−6. We apply a maximum gradient norm clipping of 10.

5
CONCLUSION
We present Gen4P, a feed-forward 4D perceiver that processes monocular video input for full 4D
reconstruction. The key to its success is to leverage prior knowledge from pretrained video generators
in a single-step manner, which differs from traditional multi-step denoising-based 4D perceivers. Our
on-demand decoder enables flexible spatial and temporal querying, while a frozen video-depth LoRA
provides a plug-in geometry prior without additional trainable parameters. Crucially, we demonstrate

that Gen4P achieves state-of-the-art and flexible 4D reconstruction, with future improvement expected
as video generative pretraining continues to advance. We also demonstrate its capability to accurately
capture dynamic motion and intricate details in real-world videos. Together, these findings support
video generators as a promising foundation for unified 4D reconstruction.

A.2
CONFIDENCE FILTERING
We use the expp1 confidence activation, i.e., c = 1 + exp(ˆc) with ˆc denoting the raw confidence
output, following VGGT (Wang et al., 2025a). This allows us to apply percentile-based confidence
filtering to remove points that the model predicts with low confidence, typically distributed at the
far-away background or foreground boundaries.
In Fig. 8, we demonstrate an application of confidence filtering. As shown in the Raw column, the
direct tracking outputs are already visually clean, but may contain a small number of flying points.
By applying confidence filtering, with a percentile as low as 2%, these flying points can be effectively
suppressed, resulting in cleaner foreground and background content.
We hereby clarify that for fair comparisons, the visualization results for any method in Fig. 4 and
Fig. 6 are NOT applied with confidence-based suppression.
B
TRAINING PREPARATION
B.1
DATASETS
We distinguish two types of input frames: encoder frames, which are fed to the encoder to extract
scene tokens, and query frames, from which local RGB patches are extracted to construct decoder
queries. The two share the same aspect ratio but may differ in spatial resolution.
For encoder frames, each raw video is maximally center-cropped to one of five predefined aspect
ratios. The cropped frames are then uniformly resized to the largest resolution within a 480 × 832
bound, resulting in five encoder frame sizes: 480 × 480, 480 × 640, 480 × 832, 352 × 832, and
256 × 832. The corresponding query frames use the same cropped content before resizing, and their
resolutions therefore depend on the native resolution of each dataset.
The resulting encoder frame and query frame resolutions are reported in Tab. A1. We preprocess each
dataset under these spatial configurations and report the approximate number of resulting subclips
for each configuration in the “Est. Subclip Count Per Size” column. During training, datasets are
sampled according to the probabilities reported in the final column. In practice, we tolerate a slight
distortion between them.
To vary the effective field of view of query patches, we apply downsizing-only augmentation to
the query frames. For each training clip, we first resize them within a 960 × 1664 bound, then
sample a side length scale factor from [0.5, 1.0], and resize all query frames by the same factor. This
augmentation is applied only to the query frames for the decoder, while the pre-encoded latents
remain unchanged.
We don’t apply random content cropping, color jittering or Gaussian blurring.
B.2
PREPROCESSING AND SAMPLING
Preprocessing. Each raw video is subsampled into 49-frame clips with frame intervals ranging from
1 to 4. We then pre-encode each clip with the Wan-VAE (Wan et al., 2025) and store the normalized
latents.
For each clip, camera poses are defined in the first-frame relative world coordinate. The 3D po-
sition GT is expressed in the tcam camera coordinate. We adopt mean normalization as in Mo-
tionCrafter (Zhu et al., 2026): valid visible 3D GT points are centered and normalized by their
mean distance to the origin. Then they are transformed to tcam’s camera coordinate system. The
displacement and normal GTs are represented in the same camera coordinate system.

The 2D position and visibility GTs in practice are both defined in the observed ttgt frame, regardless
of tcam. While a 3D point can be projected into any camera view to obtain a 2D position, visibility in
that cross-time view is not directly available from the dataset annotations. Defining both targets in

as auxiliary losses, consistent with the design in Li & Team (2026).
Frame and Query Sampling. Since the Wan-VAE uses causal temporal convolutions, the latent
at each timestamp doesn’t encode future RGB frames and thus can be safely head-truncated. Its
encoding is first-frame anchored: the first latent corresponds to the first RGB frame, while each
subsequent latent encodes four additional RGB frames. Thus, for T = 4N + 1, we slice the first T
query frames and the first N + 1 latent frames. In practice, we randomly sample the query clip length
from {21, 25, . . . , 49}. A too-short clip will fail to provide adequate context for motion learning and
lead to a performance drop.
During training, we sample at most 2048 queries per training clip, with tsrc independently sampled for
each query. Temporally, we enforce ttgt = tcam for 40% of the queries, while the rest are randomly
sampled. Spatially, 40% is sampled from the foreground and 10% is sampled from the foreground
boundaries marked by Sobel-filtered depth maps.

D.1
WORLD-COORDINATE TRACKING
Motion Threshold. ADT, DR and PO contain both static and dynamic tracks, whereas PS contains
only dynamic tracks. To reliably distinguish dynamic points from static ones for evaluation, we need
to select an appropriate motion threshold that suppresses numerical noise in static GT tracks while
preserving dynamic tracks with small motions.
We measure the motion of each GT track by accumulating adjacent-frame 3D displacements over
64 frames in meters. As shown in Tab. A4, increasing the threshold from 5 × 10−3 →1 × 10−2
changes the average static ratio only marginally from 56.82% →57.29%, indicating that 5 × 10−3
is adequate for removing the majority of the numerical noise.
In contrast, increasing the threshold from 5×10−3 →5×10−2 may lead to noticeably more wrongly
classified static tracks. The static ratio on ADT shows 67.23% →74.30%, a non-negligible 7.07%,
while on PStudio, despite being fully dynamic, increases to 2.00%.
We therefore use 5×10−3 as the dynamic-motion threshold throughout our world-coordinate tracking
evaluation.
